# Story foundation

## A Little Light Home: The Last Note

_Story premise for the first playable chapter. The title is selected; characters and setting can still change after playtesting._

### The promise to the player

You are not sent to fight a monster or save the whole world in one afternoon. You are the newest Wayfinder in a quiet meadow, helping one small, lost friend find its way home. Curiosity, care, and noticing things matter more than speed.

### The world

Starwake Meadow is a place where tiny lights drift down from the night sky. At sundown, the Hearthbell rings three warm notes. The wandering lights follow those notes home, and their glow helps the meadow's creatures find one another along familiar paths.

One stormy night, the Hearthbell rang only once. Its three star-notes scattered across the hill, the paths grew dim, and the meadow went quiet.

### The characters

- **The Wayfinder (the player):** a new helper learning how to read the meadow's paths. The player can explore alone or, in a later milestone, bring friends along.
- **Mori:** a tiny cocoon spirit with a warm inner light. Mori woke on the wrong side of the meadow and cannot remember the way home—or the tune of the Hearthbell.
- **The Hearthbell:** an old hilltop bell that calls the star-lights home. It is the visible destination for this first story beat, not yet a playable bell-ringing system.

### Chapter one: The Last Note

1. **A quiet welcome.** The opening screen explains the storm, the silent bell, and the player's role as a Wayfinder. It gives the player a clear, gentle invitation to begin.
2. **A small light in the meadow.** The player follows a stepping-stone path to Mori. The environment puts the hilltop bell in view so the destination is understandable before it is reached.
3. **A memory returns.** Greeting Mori awakens three star-lights along the path. Mori remembers the Hearthbell and points the player toward the hill. At this achievement, a small non-blocking card asks one optional, fixed-choice question about what the player and Mori should do next. Answering or skipping is saved with the browser keepsake, and that achievement will not ask again on this browser profile. The separate keepsake editor can ask for a nickname and playful favorites; those details are also optional.
4. **The next promise.** The lights lead toward the bell: the next chapter beat is to discover what scattered its three notes and help Mori ring them home.

The current build implements beats 1–3 as a short exploration slice. The hilltop bell and star-lights are a visible story hook; collecting notes, reaching/ringing the bell, saving progress, and co-op are not implemented yet.

### The player's keepsake

The optional keepsake asks only for a nickname and playful, fixed-choice preferences: a favorite color, meadow friend, and activity. The milestone question asks which gentle activity to try with Mori. Both kinds of answers are saved in this browser's local storage, are not sent to a game server, and can be forgotten from the in-game keepsake button. The player can skip either set of questions and still explore. Use preferences to make milestone dialogue and small presentation details feel familiar; never make a preference a requirement, expose it to other players, or ask a child for contact details or sensitive information.

### Play loop and emotional arc

**Notice a clue → follow it at your own pace → meet someone who needs help → make a small discovery → see the world respond.**

The first few minutes should move from calm curiosity, to companionship when Mori is found, to a clear sense of possibility when the route lights up. The player should always know what the next gentle step is, without a timer or punishment for wandering.

### Tone and presentation

- Tender, curious, and hopeful; never shout at the player or imply failure for exploring slowly.
- Use specific, memorable names and visible cause-and-effect. “Mori remembers the Hearthbell; three lights wake up” is more useful than a generic “you found a signal.”
- Keep the story readable in short sentences and familiar words while respecting children as thoughtful players.
- Use a consistent handmade-meadow visual language: soft light, rounded natural forms, a distinct path, and one character the player can recognize at a distance.
- Keep on-screen objectives and dialogue in sentence case with human, encouraging language.

### Story questions to test

- Is the Hearthbell and its three-note purpose easy to understand on a first playthrough?
- Does Mori feel like a companion worth helping, rather than a marker to walk toward?
- Do players notice the path, the three newly lit points, and the hilltop bell without being told repeatedly?
- Does the short story work for both solo play and a future cooperative group?

Ask children and their parents/guardians for feedback before locking the final tone, imagery, or reading level.
